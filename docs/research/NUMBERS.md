# Sourcing the numbers

Every figure that goes in front of an administrator needs a source that person
can check in under a minute. This is how to get them, and which of the current
ones to stop using.

---

## Retire these

**"40% turnover after freshman year."** National first-to-second-year retention at
four-year institutions runs around three quarters, so attrition is closer to a
quarter than to 40%, and at most Rhode Island four-years it is better than the
national figure. Quoting 40% to a director of institutional research is quoting
them a number they have on a dashboard, wrong, in their own building. Nothing said
after that lands.

**"$250,000 of wasted revenue per student."** Nobody pays sticker. The number an
institution actually recognises is **net tuition and fee revenue per student**,
after institutional aid, and it is a fraction of published price. Using the
published price makes every subsequent number look inflated, which is worse than
the number being large.

**Any specific dollar figure attached to the pilot's outcome.** See
[`PILOT_STUDY_DESIGN.md`](./PILOT_STUDY_DESIGN.md#the-claim-we-are-not-making).

The instinct behind all three is right. The scale of the problem is genuinely
enormous and understating it would be its own mistake. The fix is to source the
numbers rather than estimate them, because the sourced version is nearly as large
and it survives being checked.

---

## Where to get the real ones

Everything below is free and public. Pull it per institution before each meeting
and put the retrieval date on the slide.

| Figure | Source | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| First-to-second-year retention rate | IPEDS, via College Navigator or the Data Feedback Report | Search the institution on College Navigator; retention is under "Retention and Graduation Rates" |
| First-year cohort size | IPEDS Fall Enrollment | Same page, first-time full-time degree-seeking |
| Net price and net tuition revenue per student | IPEDS Finance and the Student Financial Aid survey | The Data Feedback Report puts the peer comparison on one page, which is the more persuasive artefact anyway |
| National retention and persistence benchmarks | National Student Clearinghouse Research Center, *Persistence and Retention* report | Published annually and widely cited on campus |
| Transfer-out and stop-out patterns | NSC Research Center, *Some College, No Credential* | Useful for the "they transfer with resentment" argument, which is real and documented |
| Major changing | NCES data point on undergraduate major changing (BPS longitudinal) | Roughly a third of bachelor's students change major at least once within three years; check the current figure |

The **IPEDS Data Feedback Report** deserves special mention. Every institution gets
one annually, it places them against a peer comparison group they did not choose,
and administrators know it. Framing your numbers in the same terms as a document
already sitting on their desk is worth more than any figure you could construct.

---

## The framing that works

Do not present a total. Present the arithmetic and let them supply the inputs.

> Your published first-to-second-year retention is **R%**. Your first-year cohort
> is about **N** students. So roughly **N × (1 − R)** do not come back. Every one
> of them you keep is another year of net revenue, at your own net figure. One
> percentage point of retention is **N ÷ 100** students.

Three things happen when it is put this way. The numbers are theirs, so there is
nothing to dispute. The multiplication happens in their head, which is more
persuasive than any slide. And you never claim to cause a specific improvement —
you have only shown what one point is worth, which is the number that gets a
project funded.

For a mid-sized institution with a first-year cohort in the low thousands, one
retention point is a couple of dozen students and a seven-figure lifetime revenue
line. That is enough. It does not need help.

## On the $25 seat

The seat is priced against what an institution loses, not against what the
software costs. At the arithmetic above, covering an entire first-year cohort
costs less than retaining a handful of students returns. That is the whole
argument, and it holds without any claim about effect size.

Two things to be ready for:

**"Then why is it only $25?"** Because it is an introductory price during the
pilot, and because we would rather have three Rhode Island institutions and a
publishable study than one institution and a bigger invoice. Say that plainly.
Founding-partner pricing that is honest about being founding-partner pricing reads
as confidence.

**"What will it cost later?"** Do not name a future number in a first meeting. Do
say that founding partners keep founding-partner pricing for the term of any
renewal, which is true, costs nothing to promise, and answers the real question
underneath — which is whether saying yes early gets punished.

## What to say about effect size when asked directly

You will be asked whether it works. The answer that holds up:

> We do not have a retention effect to show you, and anyone our size who claims
> one is showing you noise — you need cohorts in the thousands to see a few points
> move. What we have is an instrument built on a published protocol, a study design
> with endpoints registered before we see any data, and a comparison group. Year
> one tells you whether students finish it, whether the result is stable, and
> whether your advisors find it useful. Year two reads out persistence against the
> cohort we build with you this year. If it does not work, you will find out from
> your own institutional research office rather than from us.

That answer has closed doors on people less credible than the person saying it. It
is also the only one that survives the second meeting.
